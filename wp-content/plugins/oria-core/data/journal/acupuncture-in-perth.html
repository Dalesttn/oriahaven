Before anything else, the thing this article won't do: tell you what acupuncture is for. We don't make claims about what any practice on this directory treats, and a wellness directory is the wrong place to read about clinical outcomes anyway — that conversation belongs with a practitioner, and with your GP.

What we can be useful about is everything else, and it turns out that's most of what stops people booking: who is actually qualified, how to check, what happens in the room, what it costs, and whether your health fund will pay some of it. Perth has fourteen clinics in the directory offering acupuncture or Chinese medicine, and there's more structure behind them than most people realise.

Acupuncturists are government-registered — and you can check in a minute

This surprises people. Acupuncture isn't an unregulated corner of wellness: since 2012 it has been nationally regulated in Australia in the same framework as physiotherapy, pharmacy and general practice. Practitioners register with the Chinese Medicine Board of Australia, which sits under AHPRA — the same agency that registers your doctor.

Registration is not a certificate someone buys. General registration as an acupuncturist requires an approved four-year tertiary qualification, in the order of 3,200 hours of study including several hundred hours of supervised clinical practice. Practitioners must meet continuing professional development requirements and carry professional indemnity insurance to stay registered.

"Acupuncturist" is a protected title. So are "Chinese medicine practitioner", "Chinese herbal medicine practitioner" and "Chinese herbal dispenser". It is a criminal offence under the National Law for an unregistered person to use them, or to advertise in a way that implies they hold that registration.

Which gives you a two-minute check before your first appointment: search the practitioner's name on the AHPRA register of practitioners. It's public, free, and it will tell you whether they hold current registration and whether any conditions apply to it. Some Perth clinics publish their registration numbers openly — B&Q Acupuncture in Subiaco lists both practitioners' AHPRA numbers on its own site, which is exactly the transparency worth rewarding with your booking.

Acupuncture and dry needling are not the same registration

This is the single most useful distinction to understand, and almost nobody explains it.

Dry needling uses fine needles too, but it is a technique practised within another registered profession — commonly physiotherapy, chiropractic or osteopathy — rather than a profession of its own. A physiotherapist who does dry needling is fully registered as a physiotherapist. They are not registered as an acupuncturist, and they cannot legally call themselves one.

Neither is better. They come from different traditions, sit inside different training, and are usually chosen for different reasons. But they're often confused, and the numbers in our own directory show why the confusion is easy: alongside those fourteen acupuncture and Chinese medicine clinics, eleven other Perth practices offer dry needling — physiotherapists, chiropractors, osteopaths and remedial massage clinics from Joondalup to Mandurah.

So if you specifically want a registered acupuncturist, the word to look for on a listing is acupuncture, not needling — and the register settles it either way.

What actually happens at a first consult

A first appointment is longer than the ones that follow — commonly an hour to ninety minutes, against thirty to sixty afterwards — because most of it is conversation.

Expect a detailed intake: what brings you in, your general health history, medications and supplements, sleep, digestion, energy through the day. A Chinese medicine practitioner will typically also take your pulse at both wrists and look at your tongue — both are standard diagnostic steps in that tradition and neither is invasive.

The needling itself usually takes up less of the hour than people expect. Needles are single-use, sterile and disposable, and far finer than the ones used for injections — most are around the thickness of a hair. You'll be asked to lie down, often partly undressed and covered with a towel, and needles are placed at a number of points. Sensation varies from nothing at all to a brief dull ache as a needle is positioned. Then the practitioner usually leaves you resting for fifteen to thirty minutes with the needles in place. A lot of people fall asleep.

Some clinics add related techniques in the same visit — cupping, moxibustion, tui-na massage or electroacupuncture. TCM Acupuncture Perth in North Perth and Tao Acupuncture in Shenton Park both list several of these. If you'd rather stick to needles alone on a first visit, say so; it's a normal request.

Tell the practitioner if you're pregnant, taking blood thinners, have a pacemaker, or have a bleeding disorder. Good clinics ask anyway, and it changes how they work.

What acupuncture costs in Perth, and what your fund might pay back

Eight of the fourteen clinics in the directory publish their acupuncture fees, on a price page or inside their own online booking, and every figure below was checked on the clinic's own site on 2 October 2026. A first appointment with acupuncture costs between $95 and $200, with a median of $129. A follow-up costs $75 to $120. Call it $100.

Most of the spread is time. The dearest first visit, $200 at Thrive Wellness Centre in East Victoria Park, books seventy-five minutes, and it and Melville Osteopathy are multidisciplinary clinics where acupuncture sits beside physiotherapy or osteopathy. Take those two out and the six dedicated Chinese medicine clinics charge $95 to $140 for a first visit — the lowest is Baolin in the city, whose $95 includes the consultation.

Follow-up prices track the length of the session more than the clinic. Tao Acupuncture in Shenton Park prices by the clock: $88 for half an hour, $100 for forty-five minutes, $125 for an hour. Read every other number the same way, and ask how long it buys before you compare it with the clinic down the road.

The other six don't publish a fee online: Aligned & Centred Acupuncture, B&Q Acupuncture, Floralia Wellness, MyoFix Massage Therapy, Ping Ming Health and Yan Acupuncture. Ask when you book.

On rebates, there is a genuinely confusing bit of recent history, so here it is straight.

  • In April 2019 the Federal Government removed sixteen natural therapies from eligibility for private health insurance rebates. Acupuncture was never on that list and has remained claimable throughout.
  • From 1 July 2025, seven of those therapies were reinstated as eligible — Alexander Technique, naturopathy, Pilates, shiatsu, tai chi, western herbal medicine and yoga — after a review led by the Chief Medical Officer.
  • Nine remain excluded: aromatherapy, Bowen therapy, Buteyko, Feldenkrais, homeopathy, iridology, kinesiology, reflexology and Rolfing.

One important catch: eligible is not the same as covered. Whether your fund pays anything, and how much, is entirely its own decision and depends on your level of extras cover. Check your policy rather than assuming, and note that most clinics have HICAPS terminals so any rebate comes off at the counter instead of being claimed back later. For a sense of scale, Nature's Chinese Medicine in Belmont puts the typical rebate at $20 to $55 a treatment. That is one clinic's estimate across the funds it sees, not a promise about yours.

Acupuncture can also attract a Medicare rebate in narrow circumstances — when performed by a medical practitioner, under specific item numbers. That is not the same as seeing a registered acupuncturist, and most sessions at the clinics in this directory will not be Medicare-claimable. Your GP is the person to ask.

Where they are in Perth

The directory's acupuncture and Chinese medicine clinics cluster toward the centre — three in the CBD and two in Subiaco alone. Ping Ming Health is in the CBD on Hay Street; B&Q Acupuncture and Yan Acupuncture are both in Subiaco's medical precinct.

South of the river, TCM Health Care in Leeming requires every practitioner to have at least ten years in clinic beyond their degree, which is an unusually explicit standard to publish. If you can't get to a clinic, Ping Ming and TCM Health Care both offer telehealth consultations, which suit herbal follow-ups rather than needling for obvious reasons.

Browse them all under Acupuncture in Perth, or the wider Natural & Alternative Therapies category for naturopathy, herbal medicine and Ayurveda alongside it.

A short checklist before you book

  • Search the practitioner on the AHPRA register — two minutes, free, definitive.
  • Check the first-visit fee and how long it runs — eight clinics publish the fee, and any of them will tell you the length if you ask.
  • Check your extras cover for acupuncture specifically, and ask the clinic whether they have HICAPS.
  • Say up front if you're pregnant, on blood thinners, or have a pacemaker or bleeding disorder.
  • Allow more time than the appointment — first consults run long, and you won't want to rush out.

And the boring but necessary line: this is general information about how the profession is regulated and what a visit involves, not medical advice, and nothing here is a recommendation to use acupuncture for any condition. If you're managing a health issue, your GP is the right first conversation.
